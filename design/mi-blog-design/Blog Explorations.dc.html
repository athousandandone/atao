The Sourdough Loop

A starter is a feedback system you can eat

By Oliver Matthews · 12 July 2026 · 14 min read

A sourdough starter is the oldest continuous deployment system in the world. Twice a day, for as long as you keep it, it accepts a small commit of flour and water, runs its tests, and either rises to meet you or sulks at the bottom of the jar. There is no dashboard and no changelog. There is only the loop: observe, feed, wait, observe again.

Sourdough predates written recipes; the earliest leavened loaves are older than the alphabet that later described them.

I began keeping a starter1 the way most engineers do: as a lockdown experiment that quietly became infrastructure. What surprised me was not the bread. It was how quickly the vocabulary of my day job stopped being a metaphor. The starter is stateful. It has uptime. It has incidents, and the incidents have post-mortems, and the post-mortems produce exactly one useful artefact: a tighter loop.

Every loaf is a build artefact; the starter is the pipeline.

The professional bakery understands this in its bones. Watch a baker check a levain and you are watching someone read telemetry — smell, surface tension, the size of the bubbles at the glass. None of it is written down because all of it is felt. The recipe, when it finally appears, is just the release notes.

The loop, drawn

Strip the romance away and the system is four arrows. Flour and water go in; gas and acid come out; your senses close the circuit. The only design decision that matters is how fast you can go around.2

If you prefer your systems executable, the whole discipline compresses to a few lines. The comments are where the craft lives:

def feed(starter, flour, water):
    starter.observe()          # smell, rise, bubbles at the glass
    if starter.sluggish():
        flour *= 1.2           # feed harder, not more often
    return starter + flour + water
···

The loaf, precisely

Times assume a kitchen at 21°C. Colder rooms lengthen every step; the loop, not the clock, is the authority.

What follows is the weekend loaf as it currently ships. Treat the quantities as a lockfile and the method as documentation: pinned, reproducible, and overdue for revision the moment you disagree with it.

Ingredients

Strong white flour
450 g
Wholemeal rye flour
50 g
Water, 26°C
350 g
Ripe starter
100 g
Fine sea salt
10 g

Method

  1. Dissolve the starter in the water; add both flours and mix until no dry patches remain. Rest 45 minutes.
  2. Add the salt with a splash of water and pinch it through. The dough will briefly fall apart; this is a refactor, not a regression.
  3. Fold every 40 minutes for 3 hours, then leave until the dough has grown by half and jiggles like it means it.
  4. Shape, seam up, into a floured banneton; retard overnight in the fridge.
  5. Bake at 250°C in a covered pot for 20 minutes; uncover, drop to 230°C, and bake 22 minutes more.

Baker's percentages

Component Grams Baker's %
Flour, total 500 100
Water 350 70
Starter 100 20
Salt 10 2
Variations — rye, spelt, and the weekday loaf

Each variation changes exactly one variable, because changing two teaches you nothing.

  • Swap the rye for spelt and drop the water to 330 g; spelt is thirstier than it looks.
  • For a weekday loaf, double the starter and halve the bulk time. Faster loop, shallower flavour.
  • Ten per cent toasted seeds is the largest change that requires no other change.

A recipe, in the end, is a claim about repeatability — and repeatability is the whole game, whether you are shipping software or supper. The starter on my counter does not know it is infrastructure. It only knows the loop. Feed it well, observe it honestly, and it will hand you, every few days, the most persuasive argument I know that everything is product.
